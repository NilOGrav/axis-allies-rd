Yes. The recurring issue in the design discussions is that the **domains have gradually become more than simple subject categories**: they now influence the visual tree, Research Centres, paths, and how technologies are conceptually grouped. That makes unclear domain boundaries increasingly important.

Below is a recap of the domain-design discussion as it currently stands.

## 1. Current domain structure

The project currently uses eight domains:

| Code    | Domain                 | General purpose                                                                          |
| ------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **A**   | Air                    | Aircraft, aviation technology, air warfare                                               |
| **L**   | Land                   | Ground combat, tanks, artillery, infantry-related technology                             |
| **N**   | Naval                  | Ships, submarines, naval warfare and naval technology                                    |
| **I**   | Intelligence           | Intelligence, espionage, reconnaissance, information/warfare knowledge                   |
| **E**   | Energy And Physics     | Nuclear physics, atomic energy, advanced physics and related fundamental technologies    |
| **L&I** | Logistics And Industry | Industrial capacity, logistics, production, transportation and supporting infrastructure |
| **P**   | Programs               | Large technological/industrial programmes and special development programmes             |
| **R**   | Resource               | Physical resources/materials required for certain technologies                           |

The corresponding domain+tier combinations are used as the visual clusters in the Graphviz tree.

An important architectural decision was that **the domain is a property of the technology model**, not merely a Graphviz presentation choice.

---

# 2. What a domain is supposed to represent

The discussions established that domains should primarily represent **technological/functional areas**, rather than strict historical organizations.

For example:

* A technology being developed by an army does not automatically make it **Land**.
* A technology being developed by a navy does not automatically make it **Naval**.
* A technology involving nuclear physics does not necessarily mean that the resulting weapon belongs in **Energy And Physics**.
* A technology can support another domain without belonging to that domain.

This became particularly important when distinguishing **technology**, **resource**, and **facility**.

The rough conceptual separation is:

> **Technology** = an acquired technological capability
> **Resource** = a material/physical resource needed to support capabilities
> **Facility** = infrastructure/capacity enabling or supporting research/technology

So, for example, a uranium resource and nuclear technology should not become the same thing merely because they are historically connected.

---

# 3. Domains are not the same thing as paths

This was one of the more important clarifications.

A **domain** is the primary classification of a technology.

A **path** is an analytical technological route.

Therefore:

> Domains should be relatively stable classifications, while paths are allowed to cross domains.

A technology can belong to multiple paths.

For example, a technology may simultaneously contribute to:

* a tank-development path,
* an industrial path,
* a heavy-weapon path,

without needing to move domains merely because it participates in several technological routes.

This means we should **not solve every “this technology touches another subject” problem by creating additional domains or moving nodes around**.

---

# 4. Domains should not become historical departments

Another recurring principle was that the tree is **historically inspired, not historically limited**.

That means the question should not primarily be:

> “Which historical organization developed this?”

but rather:

> “What technological capability does this represent in the game?”

This is important because WWII research often crossed organizational boundaries.

For example:

* industrial research,
* military research,
* physics,
* weapons development,
* naval engineering,

could all overlap historically.

The tree therefore needs a **game-design classification**, not a reconstruction of WWII research ministries.

---

# 5. The Air / Land / Naval boundary

These three are the clearest domains because they correspond strongly to the major military environments.

### Air

The Air domain covers technologies whose principal capability is associated with:

* aircraft,
* aviation,
* air combat,
* air power,
* aircraft performance/armament,
* related aviation systems.

### Land

Land covers technologies whose principal capability is associated with:

* ground warfare,
* tanks,
* armoured vehicles,
* artillery,
* ground combat systems,
* related land-war capabilities.

One deliberate example from the recent discussions is **Landkreuzer P.7**.

Even though the P.7 is an enormous and unusual weapon, its placement in the **TANK path** was considered intentional. Its conceptual route therefore remains Land rather than being moved merely because it is technologically unusual.

### Naval

Naval covers:

* surface warships,
* submarines,
* naval propulsion,
* naval weapons,
* carrier technology,
* naval warfare systems.

The recent nuclear-submarine clarification is useful here:

> A **nuclear submarine** is a submarine with nuclear propulsion.

So its fundamental platform remains Naval.

A **ballistic-missile submarine**, however, is specifically a submarine capable of delivering an atomic payload. That introduces an interaction between Naval and Energy/Physics, but does not necessarily mean the submarine technology itself belongs in Energy And Physics.

This illustrates the general domain principle: **classify according to the primary capability represented by the node.**

---

# 6. Energy And Physics

This domain emerged as the natural home for technologies dealing with **fundamental or enabling physical science**, particularly:

* nuclear physics,
* atomic energy,
* nuclear weapons research,
* advanced energy technologies,
* related physical-science breakthroughs.

But an important boundary has emerged:

**Energy And Physics should not become “everything involving a nuclear weapon.”**

There is a difference between:

* the underlying nuclear/atomic technology,
* the weapon/platform that uses it,
* the resource required to produce it,
* the facility required to research/produce it.

For example:

**Nuclear physics → Energy And Physics**

while:

**Nuclear propulsion → potentially Naval when represented as a naval propulsion capability**

and:

**Nuclear submarine → Naval**

and:

**uranium/heavy water → Resource**

This layered interpretation prevents Energy And Physics from swallowing large portions of the tree.

---

# 7. Resource is deliberately separate

The Resource domain was introduced to prevent physical resources from being confused with technologies.

Examples discussed include things such as:

* uranium,
* heavy water,
* other strategically important materials.

A resource can be a prerequisite for technology without itself being a technological advancement.

This is also why the project distinguishes:

> resource requirement ≠ technology prerequisite ≠ facility requirement

even though all three can participate in eligibility.

The Resource domain therefore has a somewhat different nature from Air/Land/Naval.

That is not necessarily a problem, but it is something to keep in mind when reconsidering the domain architecture.

---

# 8. Logistics And Industry

This domain exists for the technological/economic support structure behind military capabilities.

Its conceptual area includes things such as:

* industrial production,
* logistics,
* transportation,
* manufacturing capability,
* supporting infrastructure,
* industrial processes.

The important distinction is that this domain is not simply:

> “technologies that help you produce military units.”

It represents the underlying **industrial/logistical capability** itself.

That makes it possible for an industrial technology to support Air, Land and Naval simultaneously without needing to duplicate it into all three domains.

This is one of the strongest arguments for retaining Logistics And Industry as a cross-cutting domain.

---

# 9. Intelligence

Intelligence is conceptually different from the three military domains.

It represents capabilities based around:

* information,
* intelligence gathering,
* reconnaissance,
* espionage,
* counter-intelligence,
* related information advantages.

The rationale is that intelligence is a distinct technological/organizational capability rather than simply a subcategory of Land, Air or Naval warfare.

This also makes it suitable for future modular systems involving things such as espionage.

---

# 10. Programs

**Programs** are intentionally different again.

The idea is that some entries represent **large-scale technological programmes**, rather than a conventional technology category.

This was useful for things that don't fit naturally into a single technological discipline.

A program can therefore act as a container for a major development effort without forcing the underlying concept into Air/Land/Naval/etc.

This is also consistent with the broader design principle of **modularity and optional complexity**.

However, Programs should not become a generic “miscellaneous” domain.

If something is actually a normal technology, it should ideally belong to its appropriate technological domain.

---

# 11. Research Centres complicate the domain question

Recent discussions added an important distinction.

**Research Centre** is a functional concept.

`RCENTER` is the **data-model category**.

A named facility might be something like a university, institute, directorate, etc., but those are facility identities rather than domains.

Research Centres can provide eligibility for higher-tier technologies and therefore interact with domains.

The recent idea under discussion was:

> establishing/purchasing a domain-specific Research Centre could unlock Tier 4+ technology in that domain, potentially with a one-time bonus such as a free minor unit.

That means domains may eventually have a mechanical consequence beyond visual grouping.

This makes getting the domain boundaries right more important.

At the same time, the facility should **not define the domain**. A naval research centre does not mean every technology it helps develop becomes Naval.

---

# 12. The most important general rule that emerged

Across the discussions, the best description of the intended classification logic is approximately:

> **Put a technology in the domain representing its primary technological capability, not every field it interacts with.**

Secondary relationships should normally be represented through:

* dependencies,
* paths,
* resources,
* facilities,
* modules,
* or other relationships.

This is what allows a relatively small number of domains to represent a much more interconnected technology system.

---

# 13. Why some current nodes now don't fit

This is actually an expected consequence of the tree becoming more sophisticated.

Some technologies are inherently **cross-domain**.

Typical problem cases are likely to fall into one of these categories:

### A. Platform + enabling technology

Example:

> nuclear submarine

It combines:

* Naval → submarine
* Energy/Physics → nuclear propulsion

But the node represents a **Naval platform capability**, so putting it in both domains is problematic if domain is single-valued.

### B. Weapon + scientific basis

Example:

> atomic weapon

It has:

* physics,
* energy,
* industrial,
* military,
* resource,
* facility dimensions.

But it still needs one primary home.

### C. Industrial technology with military consequences

For example, a manufacturing technology may dramatically affect:

* tanks,
* aircraft,
* ships,

while still fundamentally being an industrial technology.

This is a good case for Logistics And Industry rather than duplicating it into three military domains.

### D. Resource/technology overlap

For example:

> Heavy Water

may be essential to nuclear research but is not itself nuclear technology.

That belongs to the Resource layer rather than Energy And Physics.

### E. Programme versus technology

Some large projects don't cleanly represent a technology at all.

Those are candidates for Programs, but Programs should not become the dumping ground for awkward nodes.

### F. Technologies that are genuinely interdisciplinary

There may simply be technologies for which **one domain cannot express their nature perfectly**.

The existing architecture deliberately allows paths and dependencies to express those cross-domain relationships instead.

---

# 14. What we have *not* decided

This is important because I don't think we previously reached a final “redesign the domains” decision.

We have **not established**:

* a formal one-sentence definition for every domain;
* an exhaustive inclusion/exclusion list for every domain;
* whether any current domain should be split;
* whether any domains should be merged;
* whether cross-domain technologies should be allowed to have multiple domains;
* whether a new domain is needed;
* whether Programs should remain a domain or become another kind of classification;
* whether Resource should remain a domain or become a separate model layer;
* whether Research Centres should be domain-specific in all cases;
* a formal tie-breaker for technologies that legitimately fit two domains.

So the current problem is not evidence that the architecture is wrong. It means we have reached the point where the **domain taxonomy needs to be made explicit**.

---

# 15. The underlying design tension

I think the discussions reveal three competing goals:

```text
                 DOMAIN
                   │
        primary technological identity
                   │
       ┌───────────┼───────────┐
       │           │           │
     PATH       RESOURCE    FACILITY
       │           │           │
 strategic     material     infrastructure
 route         requirement  / eligibility
```

A technology can have relationships to all three without belonging to all three.

That is probably the cleanest conceptual model we have arrived at.

The danger is that we start asking:

> “What subjects does this technology touch?”

instead of:

> **“What is the primary capability represented by this technology node?”**

The latter is much more compatible with the existing architecture.

---

# 16. What I would do next

Rather than immediately adding/removing domains, I would make a **Domain Definition & Boundary table**.

For each existing domain:

| Domain               | Core definition | Includes | Excludes | Borderline cases |
| -------------------- | --------------- | -------- | -------- | ---------------- |
| Air                  | ...             | ...      | ...      | ...              |
| Land                 | ...             | ...      | ...      | ...              |
| Naval                | ...             | ...      | ...      | ...              |
| Logistics & Industry | ...             | ...      | ...      | ...              |
| Intelligence         | ...             | ...      | ...      | ...              |
| Energy & Physics     | ...             | ...      | ...      | ...              |
| Programs             | ...             | ...      | ...      | ...              |
| Resource             | ...             | ...      | ...      | ...              |

Then we can take **every current node that feels wrong**, classify why it feels wrong, and see whether the problem is:

1. the node's current domain,
2. an unclear domain boundary,
3. a missing domain,
4. a node that should actually be a Resource/Facility/Program,
5. or simply a legitimate cross-domain technology that needs to remain in one primary domain.

That would let us determine whether the domain architecture actually needs changing **before changing the tree itself**.

The key point from the existing design is that **we should not create a new domain merely because a technology interacts with multiple domains**. The domain taxonomy should describe primary technological identity; paths, prerequisites, resources and facilities provide the cross-domain relationships.
